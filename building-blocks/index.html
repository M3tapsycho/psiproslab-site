Building blocks

Short pieces on how your brain works. Each one stands on its own.

Mind tool: the ABC model

It's not the situation that decides how you feel, but what you think about it. This model comes from Albert Ellis (REBT) and is a core idea of cognitive behavioural therapy.

  • A, Activating event: what happens.
  • B, Belief: what you think about it.
  • C, Consequence: what you feel and do.

Example: the traffic light. You're waiting at a red light, you get distracted, and the light turns green. The driver behind you honks.

Driver 1Driver 2
A: EventSomeone honksSomeone honks
B: Thought"How impatient, it's only a few seconds!""Good, they noticed I was distracted."
C: Feeling and actionAnger, annoyed at the other driverGratitude, a friendly wave

Same situation, different thought, different outcome.

Try it yourself:

  1. Think of a recent moment when you felt a strong emotion. That's your C.
  2. Write down what happened, just the facts. That's your A.
  3. Ask yourself: "What did I think at that moment?" That's your B.
  4. Look for another possible thought about the same event. How would you feel then?

The three brains

Think of your brain as three helpers. The instinct brain keeps you alive, the emotion brain makes you feel, and the thinking brain plans and decides. This is a simple picture, not exact anatomy: the old "triune brain" model by MacLean is outdated, but the idea that stress hands control to faster systems is well supported (Arnsten, 2009 review).

Instinct is faster

Your survival reflex acts before you feel or think. Nothing is switched off; it simply wins the race.

Example: You see a garden hose in the grass and jump back. A moment later you feel fear. Only then does your thinking brain say, "It's just a hose." (LeDoux's research on the brain's fast survival circuit.)

Emotion takes over

When an emotion is very strong, it turns down your thinking brain. You act first and think later.

Example: In a heated fight with your partner, you say hurtful things you don't mean. When you calm down, your thinking brain comes back and you think, "Why did I say that?" Regret follows.

The white bear effect

Trying not to think about something keeps it in your head. Your brain can't do "don't"; it only hears the thing itself. Fighting the thought also costs energy. (Wegner's research, confirmed by a meta-analysis.)

Example: Try not to think of a white bear for one minute. It keeps popping up.

The emotional bucket

Stress and strong feelings fill a bucket inside you over the day. When it's full, even small things make it overflow.

Example: After a stressful day, a spilled glass of milk makes you snap at your kids. On a calm morning, you'd just wipe it up.

The clogged engine

Hard thinking is like a car engine working hard: dirt builds up in the filter. The more it clogs, the harder every effort feels, so you pick the easy option. A full emotional bucket is like driving uphill with a heavy load: the filter clogs faster. Rest and sleep clean it. (A 2022 brain study by Wiehler and colleagues; more studies are needed.)

Example: After a long workday you planned to cook, but you order takeaway instead.

The relief reward

Avoiding something unpleasant feels good right away. That relief works like a reward, so your brain learns to avoid it again. (Research by Sirois and Pychyl; a meta-analysis by Steel.)

Example: You open your tax forms, feel a knot in your stomach, and grab your phone instead. The knot goes away, so next time you grab the phone sooner.

TIPP: empty the bucket fast

Four quick ways to calm your body when emotions run high. (A skill from Dialectical Behaviour Therapy.)

  1. Temperature: Cold water on your face for 30 seconds.
  2. Intense exercise: A few minutes of hard movement.
  3. Paced breathing: Breathe out longer than in, for example in for 4, out for 6.
  4. Paired muscle relaxation: Tense a muscle while breathing in, let go while breathing out.

Health check: ask your doctor first if you have a heart condition, take beta-blockers or have an eating disorder.

Example: Panic rises before a meeting. You splash cold water on your face, and your heart slows down.
